Report it. Investigate it. Follow it to the world.
● For all students · in your browser · no software to install

A medicine harms a patient somewhere in Kerala this week. Whether anyone ever finds out depends on something very ordinary: whether one person filled in a form.

These practicals teach you to be that person — and then to be the team that works out what actually happened. You will not be handed a case and asked to tidy it up. You will be handed a patient, and everything else you must go and find.

07  Three things worth knowing before you come

1 · You report on suspicion, not proof. You will never be asked to prove the medicine caused the reaction. You only have to suspect it might have. Waiting for certainty is how reactions go unreported for years.

2 · What you did not ask, you do not know. The patient volunteers nothing. Writing a plausible answer into a form you never actually established is the one mistake that is marked more harshly than leaving it blank.

3 · Your assessment may have to change. Information arrives in stages, as it does in real life. Revising a judgement because new facts arrived is correct practice — it is not an admission that you were wrong.
